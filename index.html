Why it matters

Speed is the service

A route that takes less time to run can come more often with the same buses and drivers, so slow streets cost riders twice: once in the trip itself and again in the wait. Riding the whole — takes about — minutes at rush hour versus — at midday.

The city already has a plan to buy that time back. CTA and CDOT’s Better Streets for Buses plan (2023) sets out bus lanes, signal priority and stop changes, and the Bus Priority Corridor Study (October 2025) covers Pulaski, Western and Cottage Grove, three routes on this list. In 2017 the Active Transportation Alliance reported a median CTA route speed of 9.1 mph for 2015 (Back on the Bus). Its method differs from this one, so treat the comparison as context, not a trend line.

Method

How the speeds are measured

What “average speed” means here

CTA Bus Tracker reports each bus’s distance along its route pattern in feet. Speed is that distance divided by the time between GPS fixes: total miles ÷ total hours. It counts time at stops, at red lights and in traffic, which is the time a rider spends on board. It leaves out layovers at the ends of the line, where buses wait on purpose. Transit agencies call this commercial or operating speed. It is not a rider’s full trip time, which also includes walking and waiting.

The same streets in every period

Some trips only run at certain hours. Rush-hour 20 Madison buses continue into slow downtown blocks, for example, and some routes serve their outer ends more at midday. Pooling every measurement by period would mix those differences in with traffic. So each route is measured on a fixed set of ~220 meter segments observed in the morning, midday and evening. Each segment gets the same distance weight in every period. These segments cover — of the distance the 20 routes travel. Without this step, the 20 Madison would appear to slow down 9% at rush hour instead of 3%.

Speeds are pooled as total distance over total time rather than an average of segment speeds, which would overweight short, fast intervals. Each segment is assigned to a time window by its midpoint in Chicago time.

The rules, and why

    Checks

    A prototype written during profiling and this pipeline produce identical speeds before the fixed-segment step, and a reviewer re-running the pipeline route by route reproduced every figure. A different method, timing each complete trip between points half a mile from either end, agrees with the published speeds within −1.3% to +3.5% on routes 4, 9, 36, 66 and 146. The 95% intervals come from resampling the 20 service days 4,000 times.

    Limits

    • Five weeks in early fall, with school in session and no snow. Winter and summer speeds will differ.
    • No schedule data, so this cannot say whether buses run on time or how speeds compare with what the CTA plans.
    • Route speeds are bus-weighted, not rider-weighted. A slow stretch with few riders counts the same as a crowded one.
    • Detours appear as they happened. Route 8 ran on Division near Halsted, and route 47 switched to longer patterns on October 17.
    • Map cells pool both directions and every route through the cell. A cell near a terminal can show a bus turning around, and a cell at a busy stop includes boarding time.
    • The hourly charts use all service in each hour, including trips that only run at some hours.
    • The ridership cutoff is close: #20 (47th, 9,545 weekday riders) leads #21 (Fullerton) and #22 (Outer DuSable Lake Shore Express) by about 2%.
